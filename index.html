How Computers Are Connected Using Different Types of Network Topologies

A topology is the shape of a network: which computers connect to which, and how data finds its way. Pick a shape, choose two computers, and watch a message travel.

Open the Topology Lab Start with the basics

Introduction

When two computers share a file or a message, the data needs a route. How the computers, cables and devices are arranged decides the cost, speed and what happens when something breaks.

Node

Any device on the network: a computer, printer, hub or switch.

Link

The connection between two nodes, such as a cable or a wireless path.

Physical topology

How devices are actually wired and placed.

Packet

A small piece of data with sender and receiver addresses. The yellow dot in the lab is a packet.

    Comparing the six topologies

    No topology is best for everything. Each trades cost, reliability and simplicity differently.

    TopologyCablingCostIf one part failsEasy to expand?Typical use
    BusOne shared backboneVery lowBackbone break stops everyoneFair, slows as it growsSmall labs, early Ethernet
    StarOne cable per device to a centreLow to mediumOne cable fails: one device lost. Centre fails: all lostVery easyHomes, offices, schools
    RingEach device joins two neighboursMediumOne break can stop a single-direction ringFairToken Ring, fibre rings
    MeshMany links between devicesHighData finds another pathHard, many new linksInternet core, critical systems
    TreeHierarchy of starsMediumA failed switch cuts off its branchEasy, add branchesCampuses, company networks
    HybridA mix of two or moreMedium to highDepends on the parts usedVery flexibleLarge organisations

    Conclusion

    Bus is simple and cheap. Star is easy to manage. Ring gives orderly turns. Mesh is the most reliable. Tree organises growth into layers. Hybrid combines them to fit real needs. Most networks you use today are stars or trees at the edge, with mesh-like links at the core.
